How to use this list

Use the list to contrast open and closed syllables in a sorting task: students say each word, isolate the stressed syllable, and decide whether that syllable ends in a vowel or a consonant sound. The activity supplies concrete examples for later spelling or decoding work. Clinicians can target the free vowel for length or quality practice. Parents reviewing phonics homework can ask the child to find the open stressed syllable and say it alone. Poets attentive to vowel quality gain a set of words whose stressed nuclei are unblocked.

What makes a word open-syllable

An open syllable ends in a vowel sound. In “go” the single syllable is open; the vowel is free, not closed by a consonant. The same pattern appears in the stressed syllable of longer words such as “paper” (/ˈpeɪ.pər/), where the first syllable ends in the vowel sound.

A word appears on this list when its primary-stressed syllable ends in a vowel sound. The determination follows the pronunciation dictionary; spelling alone does not decide openness.

Words people commonly miscount

Tricky cases include words whose spelling suggests a closed syllable yet whose sound is open. “Table” has a stressed first syllable ending in /eɪ/ (/ˈteɪ.bəl/). “Music” ends its stressed syllable in /u/ (/ˈmju.zɪk/). “Silent” keeps an open stressed syllable (/ˈsaɪ.lənt/). “Robot” follows the same pattern (/ˈroʊ.bɑt/). These examples show the mismatch between orthographic expectations and actual vowel release.
