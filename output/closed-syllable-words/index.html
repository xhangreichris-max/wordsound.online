How to use this list

Hand the list to a group for a closed-versus-open sort: students produce the word, isolate the stressed syllable, and place the card in the closed column when that syllable ends in a consonant. The task yields ready material for short-vowel practice. Speech-language pathologists can use the blocked vowels for precision or for contrasting with free vowels. Parents can ask the child to say only the closed stressed syllable before writing the full word. Writers interested in the weight of closed syllables find a concentrated set already filtered by stress.

What makes a word closed-syllable

A closed syllable ends in a consonant sound. In “cat” the single syllable is closed by /t/. The same structure appears in the stressed syllable of “rabbit” (/ˈræb.ɪt/), where the first syllable ends in /b/.

A word is listed here when its primary-stressed syllable ends in a consonant sound. The classification is taken from the dictionary pronunciation, not from the letters on the page.

Words people commonly miscount

Words that commonly confuse the distinction include those whose spelling ends in a vowel letter yet whose stressed syllable is closed. “Have” is closed by /v/ despite the final e. “Love” ends in /v/ (/ˈlʌv/). “Give” is closed by /v/ (/ˈɡɪv/). “Come” ends in /m/ (/ˈkʌm/). These cases illustrate why the list is built from sound rather than from orthographic rules that predict openness from a final vowel letter.
